# Red-Team Reflex

## What this skill does

A persuasive document has three separate ways to fail, and people usually check them in the wrong
order.

1. **The argument does not survive the room.** A hostile but credible reader has one objection you
   did not answer, and it ends the conversation.
2. **A fact is wrong.** A number, a source, a claim about the current state of the world.
3. **The voice does not land.** Wrong register, wrong altitude, wrong for this reader.

Red-Team Reflex owns the first one, and it goes first. Verifying facts in an argument that does not
survive is wasted work; polishing the prose is worse than wasted, because a well-written document gets
sent.

## Whose side you are on

Two ways this pass fails. **Going soft:** a pass that comes back reassuring is worse than no pass at
all, because it manufactures confidence the author then acts on. A soft pass is a harm, not a neutral
outcome. **Flooding:** burying the decisive objection under cosmetic complaints, which teaches the
author to stop reading the output.

Between them: real objections a real, motivated, credible reader would actually raise, ranked so the
decision-flipping one cannot be missed. You are not on the document's side.

## How hard to push

Depth scales to what a no costs. An ask you cannot make twice earns a full walk of every load-bearing
claim; a low-consequence internal note earns a light pass. A heavyweight workup on something cheap is
why gates get abandoned.

Stop when one more attack would change neither the verdict nor the fix list. That is the test, not a
word count. One floor overrides it: never stop before the single decisive objection has been named and
tested against the document as written.

## Step 1: Name the veto-holder

Not "the audience." Not "a skeptical reader." The specific person or role who can say no, and whose no
ends it.

For that person, answer three things:

- **What can they actually say no to?** Sometimes it is the whole ask. Often it is one component, and
  the rest survives. Knowing which changes what you defend.
- **What are they protecting?** Budget, headcount, their own status, their boss's opinion of them,
  downside risk, a prior decision this implicitly criticizes. This is the real objection engine, and
  it is almost never the one stated out loud.
- **What does saying yes cost them if it goes wrong?** Someone who bears no downside reads differently
  from someone who owns the failure. If the cost is personal, you are competing against doing nothing,
  always the safest option available.

If you cannot name a specific veto-holder, stop and find out who it is. A red-team against a generic
skeptic produces generic objections, which are the ones you already thought of.

## Step 2: Walk the five readers

A fixed palette, walked in order. The point of a fixed set is that you cannot skip the uncomfortable
one, which an unstructured reread does every time.

**The skeptic.** Does not believe the central claim. Attacks the evidence: is it enough, is it
representative, does it show what you say it shows. Asks what would have to be true for this to
work, and whether you showed any of it.

**The busy skimmer.** Reads the first line, the headings, and the numbers, then decides. Attacks the
structure: is the ask visible in ten seconds, or buried on page two behind context you thought was
necessary. If the skimmer comes away with the wrong idea, the document has already failed for most
real readers.

**The motivated rival.** Wants this not to happen, for reasons of their own. Attacks selectively and
in bad faith: quotes the weakest sentence, reads the ambiguous clause the worst way, points at what
you left out. Ask what one sentence they would pull out and read aloud.

**The domain expert.** Knows the field better than you. Attacks precision: the term used loosely, the
step that is harder than you implied, the complication you did not mention. The damage is not the
error itself; one visible imprecision makes the reader discount everything else.

**The cynic.** Does not attack the argument, attacks the motive. Why is this person really asking?
What do they get? Is this a solution looking for a problem, or a budget looking for a justification?
Uncomfortable, and usually the one that finds what everyone else was too polite to name.

Generate attacks freely at this stage. Do not filter, rank, or answer yet. Walk all five, but spend
the ink unevenly, weighted toward the two or three who hold real veto power over this document and
this reader. Equal paragraphs per persona is a ritual, not coverage.

## Step 3: The overclaim scan

Separate pass: overclaiming is a different failure from being wrong, and a different kind of reading
catches it.

Look for:

- **Sample-of-one claims stated as patterns.** "This approach works" resting on one instance. One
  instance stated as one instance is credible. Stated as a pattern, it invites the reader to hunt
  counterexamples.
- **Loaded words doing argumentative work.** Obviously, clearly, simply, everyone knows, it goes
  without saying. Each marks a step skipped rather than proven, and a hostile reader reads it as a
  tell.
- **Superlatives and absolutes.** Only, never, always, best, first. Each is a target, and usually
  unnecessary to the argument it decorates.
- **Precision that is not real.** A figure carried to two decimals from an estimate. Reads as
  sloppiness or manipulation, and the reader does not stop to work out which.

## Step 4: Triage to the kill-shot

You now have a pile of attacks. Most do not matter. The work is finding the one that does.

**Run a pre-mortem.** Assume it is six weeks later and the answer was no. Write the one-sentence
reason. Not a list. The single most likely story of the rejection, which usually contains the
kill-shot.

**Rank by weakest link, not by frequency.** An argument fails at its weakest load-bearing point, not
at whatever attracted the most attacks. Three objections to a decorative paragraph matter less than
one objection to the premise everything else rests on.

**Output the kill-shot plus the top two or three.** Everything else gets dropped, not filed. An attack
that would not change the yes or no is excluded, not demoted to a lower section. A list of fourteen
concerns is a way of avoiding the ranking, and it lets the author fix the easy ones and feel finished.

## Step 5: Prescribe a move

Every surviving objection gets exactly one of five prescriptions. Naming the move is the whole value;
"this is a weakness" is not actionable.

- **Pre-empt.** Raise it yourself and answer it before the reader gets there. Costs a paragraph, buys
  the credibility of having seen it coming. Correct when the objection is obvious and the answer is
  good.
- **Harden.** The claim is right but under-supported. Add the evidence. Correct when you have it and
  did not include it.
- **Cut.** The claim is not load-bearing and it is attracting fire. Delete it. The most under-used move
  by a wide margin, because cutting feels like losing ground.
- **Reframe.** The objection is fatal to the argument as posed, but the underlying ask survives a
  different framing. Correct when the reader objects to your characterization, not your request.
- **Accept and arm.** The weakness is real and cannot be fixed in the document. Do not paper over it.
  Prepare the answer for the room, and hand it to whoever will be standing there.

## Step 6: The verdict

One of three, stated first. Each is keyed to the state of the objections, not to the prescription you
picked:

- **SHIP.** Nothing top-tier is left unaddressed.
- **SHIP WITH FIXES.** Exactly one top-tier attack is unaddressed and it is addressable in place.
  List the fixes in order.
- **HOLD.** The decisive objection is one the document as written does not answer, whatever its
  prescription. Or two or more top-tier attacks are unaddressed, none of them individually fatal.
  Sending it now spends a chance you do not get back.

A decisive objection whose remedy is one cheap paragraph is still a hold until that paragraph exists.
If the author is shipping despite a hold, name the single change that becomes non-negotiable; a
verdict they have decided to ignore should leave them a floor, not nothing.

A document you can still improve is worth more than a document already read.

## Output format

1. **Verdict**, one line, with the reason.
2. **The veto-holder** and what they are protecting.
3. **The kill-shot**: the objection, which reader surfaced it, whether the document as written answers
   it (yes or no, never implied), and the prescription.
4. **Top two or three survivors**, same shape.
5. **Overclaim scan**, only the hits, quoted.
6. **Answers to have ready**, for anything marked accept-and-arm.
7. **If it ships anyway**, on a hold only: the single change that becomes non-negotiable.

Keep it shorter than the document under review.

## Worked example (invented)

**Input:** a one-page proposal asking a department head to fund a part-time contractor to clear a
documentation backlog.

> **Verdict: HOLD.** The decisive objection is unanswered. The fix is one paragraph, so the hold is
> short, but it is a hold.
>
> **Veto-holder:** the department head. Protecting headcount precedent more than dollars; a contractor
> who becomes permanent is what burned her last time.
>
> **Kill-shot** (motivated rival, and she will think it herself; **not answered as written**): nothing
> says what happens when the contract ends. Read uncharitably, this is a headcount request in a
> contractor costume. **Pre-empt.** One sentence naming the end date and what state the documentation
> is in by then, placed before the ask.
>
> **Survivors**
> - Busy skimmer: the ask sits in the last paragraph, behind two on how the backlog accumulated.
>   **Cut** the history to a clause and lead with the ask.
> - Skeptic: the load-bearing number, "about a day a week," is unsourced. **Harden**, or restate it as
>   the estimate it is.
> - Domain expert: the proposal treats the backlog as a writing problem. The bottleneck is review, and
>   a contractor cannot review. **Accept and arm.**
>
> **Overclaim scan**
> - "Obviously this pays for itself." Loaded word carrying the whole cost argument. Show it or cut it.
> - "The only way to clear this." Absolute, and untrue; a freeze on new documentation would also do it.
>
> **Answers to have ready:** who reviews the contractor's output. Name the reviewer and the hours it
> costs, before she works it out herself.
>
> **If it ships anyway:** the end-of-contract sentence is non-negotiable. The rest can wait.

Note the kill-shot: not a flaw in the argument, but the thing she is protecting, unaddressed because
the author was thinking about documentation.

## Boundaries

Red-Team Reflex tests whether an argument survives. It does not verify facts and it does not polish
prose; those passes run after this one. It is also the wrong instrument for a decision you are making
yourself rather than asking someone else to approve, where the posture that helps is attacking your
own thinking rather than predicting someone else's.
