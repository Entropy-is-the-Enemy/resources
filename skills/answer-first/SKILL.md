# answer-first

The reader of a decision document has one question: what do you want me to know or
do? Minto's rule is that the document answers it in the first paragraph, then
explains why in groups the reader can hold in mind at once. Most drafts do the
reverse: they show the work and land the answer at the end.

This skill takes such a draft and rebuilds its order. It is a structuring pass,
not a writing pass. The words stay the author's; the sequence becomes Minto's.

Source of record: Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle* (the mind-rules, the
SCQ introduction, vertical and horizontal logic, the four ordering rules). Where
this file and the book diverge, the book wins and this file gets corrected.
House constraint: no em dashes.

## When to run

Run on the trigger phrases in the description, or when the deliverable is a
recommendation, proposal, memo, board or exec note, client finding, investor
update body, or any email that asks for a decision.

Do not run on lessons, essays, narrative, reflective writing, specs, tables,
legal or precision-fact language, or someone else's attributed writing. Those
have owners. See Boundaries.

## Intake gate (run before restructuring)

Answer three questions from the draft alone. If any answer is missing, stop and
ask the author for it in one line; never invent a governing thought.

1. **Who is the reader and what question do they walk in with?** If the draft
   does not say and context does not settle it, ask.
2. **What is the answer?** The single sentence the author would say if the reader gave
   them ten seconds. Quote it from the draft if it exists anywhere (it usually sits
   in the last paragraph). If it exists nowhere, the draft is not ready for this
   skill; say so and hand back.
3. **Is the answer a recommendation or a finding?** A recommendation ("do X")
   pyramids on the reasons to do it. A finding ("X is true") pyramids on the
   evidence. Mixed drafts get split at the top: recommendation governs, findings
   support.

## The pyramid, in the order you build it

### 1. Governing thought

One sentence. It states the answer, not the topic. "We should move billing to
annual-upfront" is a governing thought. "Billing cadence options" is a title.

Test: the reader could act on this sentence alone if forced to.

### 2. SCQ introduction

Everything before the governing thought earns its place only by making the reader
want the answer. Three moves, in order, each usually one sentence:

| Move | What it does | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | States something the reader already knows and agrees with | Reader nods |
| Complication | Names what changed or broke inside that situation | Reader asks "so what do we do?" |
| Question | The question the complication raises, stated or implied | Governing thought answers exactly this |

Then the Answer, which is the governing thought. If the draft opens with
background the reader already holds, that is Situation material: keep one
sentence of it, cut the rest. If the draft opens with the history of the
analysis, cut it entirely; process is not Situation.

### 3. Key line

The two to five reasons (recommendation) or evidence groups (finding) that
directly support the governing thought. Each key-line point is itself a full
sentence with a claim in it, never a label. "Cash" is a label. "Annual-upfront
pulls twelve months of cash forward at no discount cost" is a key-line point.

The three mind-rules apply at every level:

- **Vertical:** each point answers the question the point above it raises, and
  raises the question the points below it answer. Read down the column and check
  the question-and-answer chain holds.
- **Horizontal (kind):** points in one group are the same kind of thing. Three
  reasons and one caveat is not a group; the caveat moves to its own place.
- **Horizontal (logic):** the group is either deductive (premise, premise,
  therefore) or inductive (three instances of one class, summarized by the point
  above). Never mix in one group. Prefer inductive for a key line; readers hold
  a list of parallel reasons better than a syllogism.

### 4. MECE check on every group

Mutually exclusive: no point overlaps another. Collectively exhaustive: no reason
the reader will think of is missing. Run it group by group.

- Overlap found: merge, or split on a cleaner cut.
- Gap found: name it as a gap. Do not fill it; that is authoring. Report "the key
  line is missing a cost reason; the draft has none" and let the author supply it.
- More than five in a group: regroup. The draft has hidden a level.
- Exactly one in a group: it is not a group. Promote it or fold it in.

### 5. Order within each group

State the rule that orders every group; if no rule can be stated, the order is
arbitrary and the reader will feel it. Minto's four:

| Rule | Use when | Order |
|---|---|---|
| Deductive | The points are a chain of reasoning | Premise, premise, conclusion |
| Chronological | The points are steps in a process | First to last |
| Structural | The points are parts of a whole | The way the whole divides |
| Comparative | The points are ranked | Most important first |

Default for a key line of reasons is comparative: strongest reason first.

### 6. Support beneath the key line

Everything else in the draft either supports a key-line point or gets cut.
Assign each remaining paragraph to exactly one point. Anything that supports no
point is either a missing key-line point (flag it) or dead weight (list it under
"unplaced" and let the author decide). Never silently delete.

## Output format

Deliver in this order, every time.

**A. Findings (before any rewrite).** Report against the draft as received:

- Governing thought: quoted, and where it sat (paragraph number)
- SCQ: present, partial, or absent; which move is missing
- Key line: the points found, the logic type, MECE result (overlaps, gaps, count)
- Ordering rule per group, or "none stated"
- Unplaced material: paragraphs that support nothing

If the user asked for the audit only, stop here.

**B. The pyramid.** An indented outline, governing thought at the top, key line
beneath, support beneath that. Every node a full sentence. Proposed additions
(gaps you are naming, not filling) marked `[gap]`.

**C. The restructured draft.** The author's sentences, reordered under the pyramid, SCQ
first. Where a sentence had to change to become a claim instead of a label,
show the original in a comment or a side note so the edit is visible. Headings
optional; if used, each heading is the key-line sentence, not a topic word.

**D. Edit count.** "Moved n paragraphs, merged n, split n, relabeled n, flagged
n gaps, n unplaced." No prose summary.

## Rules

- **Structure, not content.** This skill moves, groups, orders, and relabels. It
  never adds a fact, a number, a claim, a source, or an argument. A gap is named,
  not filled.
- **Voice is untouched.** Sentence-level rewrites are limited to converting a
  label into a claim. Anything more goes to sounds-like-you after this pass.
- **Answer first is not answer only.** Keep the support; the reader who wants to
  check the reasoning must be able to.
- **Never invent the governing thought.** If the draft has no answer in it, the
  draft is not done. Say so and stop.
- **Confidentiality discipline.** Strip anything the source marks confidential or
  client-identifying before restructuring it, and say what was stripped.

## Write path

Chat output is the default. If the input is an existing file and the user asks for
the restructure in place, edit that file and nothing else. If the target file is
not named or unambiguous from context, ask; never guess a write target. Never
write to another skill's files or to any file the user did not name.

## Boundaries

- **NOT bones.** Bones strips a lesson or essay to its load-bearing moves for
  reuse in another format. This skill reorders a decision document for a reader
  who must act. A lesson goes to bones; a memo comes here. If the input is
  teaching rather than recommending, hand off.
- **NOT line-by-line.** Line-by-line builds a high-stakes deliverable from nothing,
  end-first, one walkable unit at a time. This skill needs an existing draft with
  an answer somewhere in it. If there is no draft, hand off; if line-by-line
  built the draft, this skill may run on it afterward as a check.
- **NOT draw-out.** Draw-out pulls raw material from the author before drafting. If the
  intake gate finds no answer, that is a draw-out problem, not a structure one.
- **NOT sounds-like-you or narrative-register.** Those own the sentences. This owns the
  order. Run this first; voice runs on the restructured draft.
- **NOT trace-review, airtight, or red-team-reflex.** Those gate for truth, facts,
  and hostile reading. A pyramid can be perfectly structured and wrong. Run the
  gates after.
- **NOT a deck builder.** For a slide deck, this skill produces the storyline
  (governing thought, key line as slide titles) and stops. Slide production goes
  to a slide tool or line-by-line.
- **No sub-agents.** One document, one fixed method, no fan-out value.

## Worked example (the standard)

Input (a five-paragraph memo to a CEO): paragraph 1 recaps the vendor history;
paragraph 2 lists three vendors evaluated; paragraph 3 walks the evaluation
criteria; paragraph 4 gives pricing for each; paragraph 5 ends "so we recommend
Vendor B, pending a security review."

Findings:

- Governing thought: "we recommend Vendor B, pending a security review", found in
  paragraph 5.
- SCQ: partial. Situation present (paragraph 1, too long). Complication absent;
  the draft never says why a vendor decision is needed now. Question implied.
- Key line: three reasons recoverable from paragraphs 3 and 4 (fit to criteria,
  price, implementation time). Inductive. MECE: "fit" and "criteria" overlap;
  merged. Gap: no risk reason, though the caveat about security implies one.
- Ordering: none stated. Comparative applied, price first, since the reader is the CEO.
- Unplaced: paragraph 2's list of vendors evaluated (process, not support).

Pyramid:

```
We should sign Vendor B, with the security review as the only remaining gate.
  B is the lowest total cost over three years of the three bids.
  B meets every must-have criterion; A and C each miss one.
  B implements in six weeks against A's twelve.
  [gap] Risk: security review outstanding; the draft states no other risk.
```

Restructured draft: one-sentence Situation (the contract lapses in Q4), one
sentence Complication (supplied by the author: two vendors declined to extend at current
terms), governing thought, then the three reasons in comparative order with their
pricing and criteria detail beneath, then the security caveat as its own closing
point, then the vendor list as an appendix line.

Edit count: moved 4 paragraphs, merged 1, split 1, relabeled 3, flagged 1 gap,
1 unplaced.
