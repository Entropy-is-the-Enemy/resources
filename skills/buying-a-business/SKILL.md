# Buying a Business

## What this skill does

Buying a small business has two halves. One is arithmetic: what the business earns, what it can
carry, what it is worth. The other is the play: where you found it, how you opened the conversation,
what you did on the first Monday you owned it. The arithmetic is well taught. The play is where most
first-time buyers lose, and it is mostly unwritten because it is boring.

This skill runs the play. It holds three moves that repeat across almost every small acquisition,
and says plainly when a situation is not one of them.

## Move 1: source where the competition is not

Most buyers hunt the categories that already have a name for being rolled up. That is a mistake
disguised as diligence.

**The multiple gap is a competition signal, not a quality signal.** When one category trades at eight
times earnings and an adjacent one trades at four, the usual read is that the four-times businesses
are worse. Sometimes true. More often the eight-times category has ten funded buyers chasing every
listing and the four-times category has none, so the gap measures buyer density. The buyer who can
only write one check should hunt the second category on purpose.

Run the check this way:

1. **Name the category and the adjacent one.** Not "services." Something a broker would type into a
   listing title.
2. **Count the buyers, not the businesses.** How many funds, strategics, or platforms have bought in
   this category in the last two years? Zero to two is the interesting number.
3. **Ask what the crowded category has that yours does not.** If the answer is recurring revenue,
   contracted backlog, or a regulatory moat, the gap is real and earned. If the answer is a conference
   and a newsletter, the gap is fashion.

**Time on market is leverage, not a warning.** A listing that has sat eighteen months is usually
mispriced rather than broken, and the seller has had eighteen months of being told so. Ask what
has changed in the asking price since it listed, and what the last buyer said on the way out. Both
answers are free and usually given.

The failure mode to name out loud: a listing stale from one fixable problem is an opportunity, and
one stale because the owner will not actually sell is a year of your life. Distinguish them by asking
what they will do the day after closing. A specific answer means they are selling. No answer means
they are not.

**Presume the owner-workload claim is false.** Treat whatever the listing says about how little the
owner works as unverified until somebody outside the sale tells you who runs the place on a Tuesday.
Hold yourself to a count, not a feeling: five conversations with category practitioners and three
with owners of comparable businesses, none connected to this deal. Defaults you can raise. Report the
count against the floor; under it, issue no read and say the sample is too small rather than hedge.
A category with no practitioners, retired operators, or trade body to ask cannot be checked cheaply
and scores worse for it.

**The owner is an inherited input.** You are buying the organization their behavior built, and the
people who stayed for them can leave in your first quarter. Judge no single conversation: keep a
running list across all of them, and get one read from somebody who worked alongside the owner, not
just from the owner and the broker. Report it as disqualifying, tolerable, or unknown; unknown is an
honest answer and a reason to keep asking. Clean books do not fix a company you cannot work inside.

## Move 2: open on structure, never on price

Most "we are too far apart on price" impasses are not price problems. They are wrapper problems.

Price is one number in a package that also contains timing, security, contingency, and who carries
which risk. A buyer who negotiates only the number is negotiating one variable against a seller who
has five. So lead every conversation with the wrapper:

> "Before we talk about the number, help me understand what you need the structure to do. Is this
> about the total, about what you take home at close, or about what happens to the people?"

That question does three things. It surfaces which variable the seller actually cares about, which is
frequently not the headline. It turns a single contested figure into a set of tradeable ones. And it
makes a later counter feel like problem-solving rather than a haircut.

**Never put the first number on the table.** Not a price, not a range, not a ballpark. Whoever names
a figure anchors everything after it. Asked point blank what you would pay, hand it back: "I'm not
there yet. Which part of this do you need room on, the total or the timing?" Then stop talking. The
silence is part of the move.

**The reframe that closes gaps.** Before you walk from a price you cannot pay, check all four:

- **Structure.** What form does the consideration take? Cash at close, paper carried by the seller,
  an amount held back and released on conditions, equity retained.
- **Terms.** When does each piece pay, at what rate, and in what order relative to the lender? Paper
  behind the bank paying interest only for two years is a different instrument from paper that
  amortizes from month one.
- **Price.** The headline. Adjust this last, because it is the variable the seller has emotional
  history with.
- **Partners.** Who else is at the table? A co-investor, a second lender, or the seller staying on in
  a defined role can each turn an unaffordable deal into a financeable one.

If the flaw sits in the wrapper, the correct output is a counter, not a pass. Reserve a hard pass for
a business that fails on its own merits, not for one that failed at a structure you proposed.

**A lower price can be the more expensive deal.** All-cash at a discount that leaves nothing for the
first bad quarter is worse than a higher headline with paper on standby behind the bank. Test every
structure: in the worst realistic first year, does this still service its debt? A structure that
only works in the good case has not been structured.

## Move 3: run the first hundred days off one page

**If it grows past one page, it is a wishlist, not a plan.** A page is what a new owner can execute
while also answering the phone. Everything that does not fit goes in the twelve-month plan.

The sequence matters more than the contents: each step buys the information the next one needs.

| Weeks | Move | Why it is here and not later |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Financial systems audit | The seller's reporting was built for the seller. Get to a weekly cash view before anything else. |
| 2 to 3 | Top customer calls | The concentration you underwrote is a spreadsheet fact until a customer tells you they stayed for the old owner. |
| 3 to 4 | Churned customer calls | The cheapest diligence available, and only available now. People who left tell a new owner things current customers will not. |
| 4 to 7 | Employee one-to-ones, two layers deep | Not just direct reports. The second layer knows where the work actually happens and has never been asked. |
| 8 to 10 | Cash dashboard, three to five numbers | Built from what the first seven weeks taught you, not a template chosen in advance. |
| 11 to 13 | The twelve-month plan | Written last, because now it is informed. |

Two rules that keep the page honest:

- **Announce nothing structural in the first thirty days.** You do not yet know which inefficiency is
  load-bearing. The rule you delete in week two is the one that was preventing something.
- **Answer the phone yourself at first.** Route inbound to a number you pick up personally. It is the
  highest-density customer research available and it expires the moment you delegate it.

## Output format

```
SOURCING READ
  Category: <the one a broker would type>
  Adjacent crowded category: <name> at <multiple, if known>
  Buyer count, last 24 months: <n>
  Gap verdict: earned / fashion / unknown, because <reason>
  Time on market: <n months>  ->  <leverage or warning, and the question that settles it>
  Owner-workload claim: <their words>  ->  corroborated by <outsider> / unverified
  Outside conversations: <n> practitioners / <n> peer owners (floor 5 / 3), ecosystem reachable?
  Owner flags: disqualifying / tolerable / unknown, because <reason>

STRUCTURE FRAME
  What the seller said they need: <in their words>
  First number named by: <buyer | seller>, at <figure>
  What that suggests they actually care about: <structure | terms | price | people>
  Levers available: <the ones live in this deal>
  Worst-realistic-year test: <passes / fails> because <reason>

HUNDRED-DAY PAGE
  Wk 1      <financial systems>
  Wk 2-3    <top customers: names or segments, count>
  Wk 3-4    <churned: source of the list>
  Wk 4-7    <one-to-ones: headcount, layers>
  Wk 8-10   <the three to five numbers>
  Wk 11-13  <the twelve-month plan>
  Cut to the twelve-month plan: <everything that did not fit>

OPEN QUESTIONS
  <what this read needed and did not have>
```

## Worked example (invented)

**Input:** a buyer looking at a family-owned commercial bakery supplying regional grocery
chains. Listed nineteen months ago. Asking price is roughly a third above what the buyer's
lender will support. The seller has said twice that the number is the number.

```
SOURCING READ
  Category: wholesale bakery, grocery channel
  Adjacent crowded category: contract food packaging, trading much higher
  Buyer count, last 24 months: 1 (regional strategic, one tuck-in)
  Gap verdict: partly earned. Packaging carries multi-year supply contracts; this bakery
    sells on annually renewing purchase orders. Real difference, not fashion. But one buyer
    in two years means no auction dynamic.
  Time on market: 19 months  ->  leverage. Asking price has not moved once. The question
    that settles it: what did the last buyer say on the way out?
  Owner-workload claim: "owner works mornings"  ->  unverified. A retired competitor says
    he still opens the floor at four. Nobody outside the sale has confirmed either version.
  Outside conversations: 6 practitioners / 3 peer owners (floor 5 / 3). Ecosystem
    reachable: yes, an active regional bakers' association.
  Owner flags: tolerable. Two people describe a temper on delivery misses; nobody
    describes turnover. Unresolved, keep asking.

STRUCTURE FRAME
  What the seller said they need: "the number is the number"
  First number named by: seller, at the unchanged asking price
  What that suggests they actually care about: price, but the repetition and the frozen
    asking price suggest a fixed post-sale obligation behind it. Ask what the proceeds
    are for.
  Levers available:
    Structure - seller paper for the gap, sitting behind the bank
    Terms - interest-only on that paper through the first two years
    Price - hold the headline the seller has repeated, which is the point
    Partners - seller stays on for the grocery relationships, paid, defined, time-boxed
  Worst-realistic-year test: passes. Losing the largest grocery account in year one still
    covers the bank, because the seller paper is on standby and does not amortize yet.
    A lower all-cash headline fails the same test.

HUNDRED-DAY PAGE
  Wk 1      Weekly cash view; ingredient cost per unit by line; who signs what
  Wk 2-3    Four grocery buyers, in person, at their offices
  Wk 3-4    Two accounts lost in the last 24 months; list from the sales log
  Wk 4-7    Nineteen one-to-ones: plant, drivers, office. Night shift included.
  Wk 8-10   On-time delivery, waste percentage, cash conversion days, cost per unit
  Wk 11-13  Twelve-month plan
  Cut to the twelve-month plan: the second oven, the direct-to-consumer idea, rebranding

OPEN QUESTIONS
  Whether any grocery relationship is personal to the seller rather than institutional.
  The top-customer calls answer this; nothing before them does.
```

The structure frame is the load-bearing part here. Nothing about the price moved. The deal
became financeable anyway.

## Boundaries

This skill runs the play, not the arithmetic. It does not value a business, compute debt capacity,
build a model, read financial statements, or issue a buy or pass verdict. Hand it a deal that has
cleared a numbers review; if it has not, say so and stop.

It does not practice law or replace counsel. Every structure here becomes real only in a document
someone qualified has drafted and both sides have read.

It will not manufacture a hundred-day plan for a business it knows nothing about. A generic
page is worse than no page, because it looks like preparation.
