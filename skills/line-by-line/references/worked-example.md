# Worked example: an end-first tutorial deck

A concrete walk of line-by-line, building a 60-minute hands-on tutorial for a small team of near-beginners learning a new tool. Details are generic; the point is the build order and the corrections the method made cheap.

## The setup

- Audience: a handful of team leads with mixed, mostly low experience with the tool. The weakest had barely opened it.
- Constraint: 60 minutes, one session, no guaranteed follow-up.
- The rule that governed everything: design every step for the weakest starter in the room.

## Step 1: end first (the last unit, built first)

The end goal was pinned down precisely with the owner: by the end, each attendee would (a) understand the key distinction the tool turns on, (b) know when it is worth using, (c) have named a few real tasks worth handing off, and (d) have started building the first one in the room. That arrival state was built as the closing slide, so every earlier slide had a fixed target to aim at. The owner defined the end in their own words; the method did not guess it.

## Step 2: the start (first unit after the cover)

The true starting point was an honestly mixed, low-experience room. The opening slide named that range gently, without singling anyone out, so the least-prepared person felt safe to stay. Not an agenda, not a hook: an honest picture of where the audience actually was. The first draft was softened once, on the owner's note, so no individual could feel called out.

## Step 3 and on: the most walkable step, repeated

Each next slide was the single most walkable step from where they were toward the end, not the most impressive one:

- First, the one core distinction the tool turns on, taught at the lowest possible floor.
- Then the concept that makes the tool actually pay off, framed to relieve beginner anxiety rather than raise it.
- Then a simple test for which work is worth handing to the tool.
- Then a map of what could be built, then the building blocks one at a time, each taught in full but paced fast.
- Then a working moment: name your own tasks. Then: start building one, now, in the room.

Each unit was fully agreed with the owner before the next was drafted: content in, content out, does it earn its place.

## Grounding as we went

Every external claim was verified against primary sources at the time each slide was built, not saved for a later pass. Facts that change often were flagged to re-verify close to the delivery date.

## The gate

A final pass checked, in order: facts sourced, the argument survives a skeptical attendee, then tone and phrasing last, then a final scan for anything that should not ship. The owner held the final call on shipping.

## Why front-to-back would have failed

Building start-to-finish would have locked the structure before the destination and the true starting point were fixed. Several times the owner corrected the path: one step assumed knowledge that had not been taught yet; one concept needed to move earlier. Because units were built one at a time against a fixed end and a real start, those corrections were cheap, a single slide reworked. Built front-to-back, the same problems would have surfaced only at the end, after everything downstream was already resting on the wrong assumption.

## The transferable lesson

The destination and the honest starting point are the two hardest and most important things to get right, so build them first, literally, as the last and first units. Then the middle is just a sequence of walkable steps, each one checked against the weakest member of the audience. Momentum is not the goal. A path that holds is.
